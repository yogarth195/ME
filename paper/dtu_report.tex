\textbf{\Large ABSTRACT}\\[4pt]
\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.4pt}
\end{center}

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords---} Neurosymbolic Artificial Intelligence, Resume--Job Description Matching, Skill Ontology Graph, Sentence-BERT, XGBoost, SHAP Explainability, Applicant Tracking System, Natural Language Processing, Information Retrieval

\vspace{12pt}

Automated resume screening represents one of the most consequential yet technically underdeveloped applications of artificial intelligence in modern industry. Existing Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS), which filter the majority of job applications before any human review, rely fundamentally on keyword co-occurrence and rule-based scoring. This approach suffers from two well-documented and critical failures: \textit{semantic blindness}, wherein semantically equivalent skill descriptions fail to match due to surface-form differences, and \textit{decision opacity}, wherein neither candidates nor recruiters can obtain any structured justification for a screening outcome. These failures have measurable real-world consequences --- qualified candidates are systematically eliminated based on phrasing choices, and recruitment processes lack the auditability required by emerging regulatory frameworks around algorithmic decision-making in employment.

This report presents \textbf{ExplainHire}, a neurosymbolic artificial intelligence system for explainable resume--job description matching. ExplainHire is organized as a seven-layer sequential pipeline that addresses both failures simultaneously. The system integrates three complementary matching signals: (1) a \textbf{symbolic skill ontology graph} comprising 434 nodes and hierarchical IS-A relationships derived from the O*NET occupational database, enabling six-level weighted graph traversal that captures exact matches, alias matches, and ontological proximity relationships between skills; (2) a \textbf{domain-adapted SBERT sentence embedding model} (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) fine-tuned on 887 real resume--job description pairs drawn from 144 genuine B.Tech student resumes matched against 17 real industry job descriptions manually collected from Naukri and LinkedIn, covering roles spanning Full Stack Development, Data Science, Backend Engineering, Flutter Development, Machine Learning Engineering, and Golang Development; and (3) \textbf{structural feature signals} capturing years-of-experience gap, education level gap, and resume section coverage. These three signals are decomposed into 14 numeric features and fused by an XGBoost classifier trained on 1,854 labeled resume--job description pairs assembled from synthetic, Kaggle, and real-world sources.

Explainability is delivered through two mechanisms: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) TreeExplainer provides feature-level attribution for every prediction, while a sentence-level evidence mapper identifies the precise resume sentence supporting each matched skill claim. A skill gap ranker computes counterfactual score improvements for each missing skill and presents them in ranked order with curated learning resource links.

ExplainHire achieves a five-fold stratified cross-validated F1 score of \textbf{0.9045} and a full-dataset F1 of \textbf{0.951}, representing an improvement of \textbf{+11.86\%} over the best single-ablation configuration (Graph + SBERT without structural features, F1 = 0.832). On the public ResuméAtlas benchmark dataset (Heakl et al., 2024), containing 13,389 resumes across 43 job categories, ExplainHire achieves a macro F1 of \textbf{0.720}, outperforming the reported TF-IDF baseline of 0.61 by \textbf{+11.06\%}. ExplainHire achieves the highest reported F1 among all comparable published systems and is, to our knowledge, the first resume--job matching system to provide structured, auditable, sentence-level explanations combined with ranked skill gap analysis.

\chapter{INTRODUCTION}

\section{Overview and Motivation}

The modern job market operates at a scale that fundamentally exceeds human processing capacity. In India alone, Naukri.com reports receiving over 80 million registered job seekers interacting with millions of active job postings at any given time. LinkedIn India hosts over 100 million professionals, and the volume of resume submissions for a single corporate job posting routinely exceeds several hundred applications within the first 48 hours of posting. This volume has made automated resume screening not merely convenient but operationally necessary for most organizations of scale.

The dominant technology used for this screening is the \textbf{Applicant Tracking System (ATS)} --- software platforms such as Workday, Greenhouse, iCIMS, and their Indian equivalents built into Naukri and LinkedIn Recruiter. These systems parse resumes into structured fields and score candidate profiles against job descriptions using keyword frequency and rule-based matching. The fundamental algorithm underlying most commercial ATS products has not changed substantially in two decades: count how many words from the job description appear in the resume, apply weights to preferred terms, and rank candidates accordingly.

This approach is deeply flawed. Research consistently shows that ATS systems eliminate between 75\% and 88\% of applicants before any human review --- and that a significant fraction of those eliminated candidates would have been strong matches had the system understood the content of their resumes rather than merely their surface text. The problem is not lack of computational power; it is an architectural limitation. Keyword matching is \textit{semantically blind} and \textit{completely opaque}. A system that scores ``built distributed backend services with Go'' as zero against a job description requiring ``Golang developer with microservices experience'' has not understood anything --- it has merely counted words.

Beyond the candidate experience, this opacity creates systemic problems for employers. Hiring decisions made by black-box systems cannot be audited, cannot be explained to candidates who request justification, and increasingly cannot comply with emerging regulations around algorithmic accountability in employment. The European Union's AI Act, for example, classifies employment-related AI systems as ``high-risk'' and mandates explainability, human oversight, and documentation of decision logic. Indian regulatory frameworks around workplace discrimination are evolving similarly.

\textbf{ExplainHire} is our response to this problem. It is a \textbf{neurosymbolic artificial intelligence system} that combines the structured reasoning capability of symbolic AI (a 434-node skill ontology graph derived from the O*NET occupational database) with the semantic language understanding capability of neural AI (a fine-tuned SBERT sentence embedding model trained on real recruitment data) and the learned fusion of a gradient-boosted classifier (XGBoost). The result is a system that not only predicts match/no-match with state-of-the-art accuracy (F1 = 0.951) but provides complete, structured, sentence-level explanations for every decision it makes.

\section{Problem Statement}

The technical problem addressed by ExplainHire can be formally stated as follows.

Given:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A resume document $R$ in PDF or DOCX format, containing unstructured text describing a candidate's skills, work experience, education, and projects
  \item A job description $J$ as plain text, specifying required skills, years of experience, educational qualifications, and role responsibilities
\end{itemize}

\noindent Produce:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A binary classification label $\hat{y} \in \{0, 1\}$ where $\hat{y} = 1$ indicates that the candidate represented by $R$ is a suitable match for the role described by $J$, with an associated confidence probability $p \in [0, 1]$
  \item An explanation structure $E$ containing:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item For each skill $s \in S_J$ (required by the JD): whether it was matched, at what ontological distance, and if matched, the specific sentence in $R$ that provides evidence of the skill
      \item A ranked list of missing skills $s \in S_J \setminus S_R$, ordered by their estimated counterfactual impact on the match score
      \item A SHAP feature attribution vector indicating which of the 14 input features were most influential in the classification decision
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

This is treated as a supervised binary classification problem over a structured 14-dimensional feature space, with explainability generated through a combination of SHAP attribution and rule-based evidence extraction.

\section{Research Contributions}

The specific research contributions of this project are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A neurosymbolic matching pipeline}: The first resume--job matching system to simultaneously employ symbolic ontology graph traversal, fine-tuned neural sentence embeddings, and structural feature signals, fused by a learned classifier. No prior published system combines all three signal types.

  \item \textbf{A domain-specific skill ontology}: A 434-node directed graph of skill relationships derived from the O*NET occupational database and extended with technology-specific nodes relevant to Indian software engineering hiring contexts, with a complementary 800-entry alias normalization table.

  \item \textbf{Domain-adapted SBERT for hiring}: Fine-tuning of \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} on 887 real resume--job description pairs from genuine B.Tech student resumes and industry job postings, adapting the model to the specific semantic patterns of technical hiring.

  \item \textbf{Structured explainability}: Sentence-level evidence mapping that identifies the exact resume sentence proving each matched skill, combined with counterfactual skill gap analysis that tells candidates specifically what to learn and in what order.

  \item \textbf{State-of-the-art performance with public validation}: F1 = 0.951 on our dataset (CV F1 = 0.9045) and F1 = 0.720 on the public ResuméAtlas benchmark, with full ablation study validating the contribution of each component.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

ExplainHire is designed and validated for technical and software engineering job roles. The ontology and alias table cover technology skills relevant to roles in software development, data science, machine learning, and related fields. Extension to non-technical domains (law, medicine, finance) would require ontology expansion and new fine-tuning data.

The system accepts resume text in English and processes job descriptions written in English. Multi-lingual support is a direction for future work.

Classification is binary (match/no-match). Ranking across multiple candidates is achievable by comparing confidence scores but is not the primary evaluated task.

\chapter{BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK}

\section{The Recruitment Technology Landscape}

Modern recruitment is mediated by software at virtually every stage. A typical hiring pipeline at a large organization proceeds as follows: a recruiter posts a job description on platforms such as Naukri, LinkedIn, or the company careers portal; candidates submit resumes through an online form; the ATS parses and indexes all submissions; automated filters eliminate candidates below a threshold; the surviving pool is reviewed by human recruiters; shortlisted candidates proceed to interviews.

The ATS filter is the critical bottleneck. Industry surveys conducted by Jobscan and Resume Worded consistently find that 75--88\% of applicants are eliminated at the ATS stage, before any human has read their application. For a competitive role receiving 500 applications, this means approximately 375--440 candidates are rejected by an algorithm. The accuracy of this algorithmic filter therefore has enormous consequences --- not merely for individual candidates but for the quality of talent pools that organizations build.

Despite this consequence, the core technology underlying most ATS products remains primitive. The standard approach is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Parse resume into fields (name, education, skills, work history)
  \item Extract keywords from the job description
  \item Score each resume by the count and weight of keyword overlaps
  \item Rank candidates by score and apply a cutoff threshold
\end{enumerate}

This approach has a well-understood fundamental flaw: it is \textbf{lexically sensitive} but \textbf{semantically blind}. It treats language as a bag of tokens with no understanding of meaning, context, or conceptual relationships. A candidate who writes ``developed machine learning models for fraud detection'' scores zero against a JD that requires ``AI engineer with experience in anomaly detection,'' despite the two descriptions being substantively equivalent.

\section{Prior Work in Resume--Job Matching}

\subsection{Neural Representation Learning Approaches}

\textbf{PJFNN (Zhu et al., 2018)} \cite{zhu2018} introduced the Person-Job Fit Neural Network, one of the earliest deep learning approaches to resume--job matching. PJFNN uses a hierarchical Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to learn joint representations of resumes and job descriptions from user click behavior logs collected from the Baidu recruitment platform. By training on implicit feedback from millions of candidate-recruiter interactions, the model learns to embed resumes and job descriptions in a shared semantic space where matching pairs cluster together. PJFNN achieves F1 = 0.800 on its proprietary dataset. However, it requires large volumes of implicit feedback data that are unavailable to most practitioners, provides no explanation for its decisions, and does not capture structured skill relationships.

\textbf{Li et al. (2020)} \cite{li2020} proposed a BERT-based model with multi-head attention for collaborative recruitment matching on the CRC (Collaborative Recruitment Corpus) dataset. The model applies BERT to encode resume and job description text, then uses multi-head attention to compute cross-document interactions. Reported accuracy is 0.792 on that dataset. While BERT provides substantially better language understanding than CNN approaches, the model remains a black box --- attention weights in BERT are not reliably interpretable and do not provide structured explanations.

\subsection{Graph and Multi-View Approaches}

\textbf{Bian et al. (2020)} \cite{bian2020} presented a multi-view co-teaching network for job--resume matching that combines graph neural network representations with BERT text representations. The system constructs co-occurrence graphs from candidate and job description tokens and applies GNN message passing to capture structural relationships. The multi-view fusion achieves approximately F1 = 0.76--0.80 on private datasets. This is the closest architectural precedent to ExplainHire in that it combines graph and neural signals; however, the graph component is co-occurrence based rather than a structured domain ontology, and no explainability is provided.

\subsection{Sentence Embedding Approaches}

\textbf{conSultantBERT (Lavi et al., 2021)} \cite{lavi2021} fine-tuned Sentence-BERT on the proprietary Randstad recruitment dataset using a Siamese network architecture to learn similarity between job titles and candidate profiles. ConSultantBERT achieves F1 = 0.749 on the Randstad dataset. This is the most directly comparable work to our SBERT fine-tuning component. The key differences are: (1) conSultantBERT fine-tunes on a proprietary dataset that cannot be reproduced or extended; (2) it does not combine neural embeddings with symbolic ontology signals; and (3) it provides no explanation for its similarity scores.

\subsection{Benchmark and Baseline Work}

\textbf{Heakl et al. (2024)} \cite{heakl2024} introduced the ResuméAtlas dataset, comprising 13,389 resumes across 43 job categories, and established a TF-IDF + XGBoost baseline achieving macro F1 = 0.61 on this dataset. ResuméAtlas is the only public large-scale resume dataset with labeled job category annotations among all prior work we reviewed. All other datasets used in the papers cited above are private and unavailable for external validation. This makes ResuméAtlas uniquely valuable as an independent benchmark, which is why we use it for external validation in Section 5.2.

\section{Neurosymbolic Artificial Intelligence}

Neurosymbolic AI refers to architectures that combine the complementary strengths of symbolic and neural computing paradigms. The distinction between the two paradigms is as follows:

\textbf{Symbolic AI} systems represent knowledge as explicit structures --- rules, graphs, ontologies, logic programs. They are interpretable by design: every inference step can be traced, every conclusion has an explicit justification. However, they are brittle to input variation: if the input does not match the expected form, the system fails. They cannot generalize from data or handle the inherent ambiguity of natural language.

\textbf{Neural AI} systems learn representations from data through gradient optimization. They generalize remarkably well across input variations and handle natural language with unprecedented capability. However, the learned representations are distributed across millions of parameters with no human-interpretable structure. Decision-making is opaque.

Neurosymbolic integration has been an active research area since the early 1990s, experiencing renewed interest with the rise of deep learning. In ExplainHire, the integration is direct and purposeful: the ontology graph provides structured, interpretable skill reasoning (the symbolic component); SBERT provides semantic language understanding (the neural component); XGBoost provides a learned fusion that discovers the optimal weighting of both signals from labeled data; and SHAP provides post-hoc explanation of the learned fusion. This architecture ensures that every decision has both a statistically learned justification (SHAP values) and a human-readable structural justification (ontology traversal paths and evidence sentences).

\section{Explainability Methods}

\subsection{SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)}

SHAP \cite{lundberg2017} is a model-agnostic framework for explaining machine learning predictions. It is grounded in cooperative game theory: the Shapley value of a feature $i$ for a prediction $f(x)$ is defined as the average marginal contribution of feature $i$ across all possible feature orderings:

\begin{equation}
\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!} \left[ f_{S \cup \{i\}}(x_{S \cup \{i\}}) - f_S(x_S) \right]
\end{equation}

where $F$ is the full feature set and $f_S$ is the model prediction using only features in subset $S$. SHAP TreeExplainer computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles including XGBoost in polynomial time, making it computationally feasible for production use.

\subsection{Ontology-Based Explanation}

Independent of SHAP, the skill ontology provides a structurally different form of explanation: the traversal path that produced each match score. When the system determines that a candidate's resume skill ``pytorch'' matches the JD requirement ``machine\_learning'' via a child-to-parent hop, this relationship is both the computational evidence for the match and a human-intelligible explanation: ``You have PyTorch, which is a deep learning framework, which is a type of machine learning --- the JD requires machine learning.'' This form of explanation is not available in any purely neural system.

\section{Design Principles}

The ExplainHire architecture is guided by four design principles:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Separation of concerns}: Each layer of the pipeline has a single, well-defined responsibility. This makes individual components testable, replaceable, and independently improvable.
  \item \textbf{Signal independence}: The three matching signals (graph, semantic, structural) are computed independently before fusion. This ensures that the failure or weakness of any one signal does not corrupt the others.
  \item \textbf{Explainability by design}: Explanation is not a post-processing afterthought but an architectural requirement. Every component that contributes to a decision also contributes to its explanation.
  \item \textbf{Graceful degradation}: The system produces a meaningful output even when individual signals return edge-case values (e.g., a resume with no extractable skills still receives a SBERT score and structural score).
\end{enumerate}

\section{L1 — Input Validation}

The input validation layer is the first line of defense against malformed or unprocessable inputs. It performs the following checks in sequence:

\textbf{File type validation}: Only PDF and DOCX files are accepted. File type is validated by inspecting the file's magic bytes (not merely its extension), preventing trivial bypass attempts.

\textbf{File size validation}: Files exceeding 5 MB are rejected. Most well-formatted resumes are well under 1 MB; the 5 MB limit accommodates graphics-heavy resumes while preventing resource exhaustion.

\textbf{Text extractability}: The resume is parsed for machine-readable text content. Scanned image PDFs, which contain pixel data with no embedded text layer, are detected and rejected with an informative error message directing the candidate to upload a text-based PDF.

\textbf{Minimum content check}: Extracted text must meet a minimum character count threshold to ensure that the downstream pipeline receives sufficient content for meaningful feature extraction.

\textbf{JD validation}: The job description input is validated for non-emptiness and minimum length before processing begins.

Any validation failure returns an HTTP error response with a user-facing message explaining the specific issue and how to resolve it.

\section{L2 — Parsing}

The parsing layer extracts structured data from both the resume and the job description. PDF text is extracted using PyMuPDF (fitz), which preserves text ordering and handles multi-column layouts more reliably than many alternatives. DOCX text is extracted using python-docx.

\subsection{Resume Parsing}

From the resume text, the parser extracts:

\textbf{Skills}: A vocabulary-based scan identifies all skill mentions. The vocabulary covers all canonical node names in the ontology plus all surface forms in the alias table. Skills are extracted as a list of raw strings; normalization occurs in L3.

\textbf{Years of Experience}: A suite of regex patterns extracts YOE from the resume text:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pattern ``\texttt{[0-9]+\textbackslash{}s*\textbackslash{}+?\textbackslash{}s*year}'' matches expressions like ``3 years'', ``3+ years'', ``3 years of experience''
  \item Pattern ``\texttt{[0-9]+\textbackslash{}s*to\textbackslash{}s*[0-9]+\textbackslash{}s*year}'' matches ranges like ``2 to 4 years''
  \item When multiple matches are found, the maximum is taken as the candidate's YOE
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Education Level}: Degree keywords are searched in the resume text and mapped to a numeric rank: High School (1), Diploma (2), B.Tech/B.Sc/B.E. (3), M.Tech/M.Sc/M.E./MBA (4), PhD/Doctorate (5). The highest detected rank is used.

\textbf{Section Coverage}: The parser detects the presence or absence of standard resume sections by searching for section header keywords: ``skills''/``technical skills'', ``experience''/``work experience'', ``projects''/``academic projects'', ``education''/``qualifications''. The fraction of these four sections present constitutes the section coverage score.

\subsection{Job Description Parsing}

The same techniques are applied to extract required skills, required YOE (from phrases such as ``minimum 3 years'', ``3+ years required''), and required education level from the job description text.

\section{L3 — Normalization}

The normalization layer bridges the gap between the natural language variation in resume text and the controlled vocabulary of the skill ontology. This gap is the primary reason simple keyword matching fails: there are hundreds of ways to express the same skill in a resume, and an ontology-based system cannot reason about them without first resolving this ambiguity.

The \textbf{alias table} is a JSON file containing 800+ entries mapping surface forms to canonical ontology node identifiers:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
{
  "nodejs":        "node.js",
  "node js":       "node.js",
  "node.js":       "node.js",
  "Node.JS":       "node.js",
  "reactjs":       "react",
  "react.js":      "react",
  "ML":            "machine_learning",
  "deep learning": "deep_learning",
  "pytorch":       "pytorch",
  "PyTorch":       "pytorch"
}
```
\end{lstlisting}

The alias table was constructed through a manual review of all 144 real resumes in our dataset. Every distinct spelling variant observed was added as an alias entry. This process ensures near-complete coverage of the surface forms that appear in real B.Tech student resumes in Indian technical hiring contexts.

After normalization, all skill mentions from both the resume and the JD are represented as canonical ontology node identifiers, enabling consistent graph traversal in L4a.

\section{L4 — Matching Layer}

The matching layer computes three independent signals from the normalized resume and JD representations. These signals capture fundamentally different aspects of candidate--job compatibility.

\subsection{L4a — Graph Matcher (Symbolic AI)}

\subsubsection{Ontology Graph Structure}

The skill ontology is a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ where each node $v \in V$ represents a canonical skill and each edge $(u, v) \in E$ represents an IS-A relationship (``$u$ is a type of $v$''). The graph contains 434 nodes and was constructed from two sources:

\textbf{O*NET Database}: The Occupational Information Network (O*NET), maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor, provides a hierarchical taxonomy of occupational skills, knowledge areas, and abilities. We extracted the technology-relevant subtrees from O*NET and translated them into a NetworkX DiGraph structure.

\textbf{Manual Extension}: O*NET's technology coverage is biased toward U.S. occupational contexts and does not include many skills that are central to Indian software engineering hiring. We manually added nodes for frameworks, tools, and technologies that appear in our 17 real job descriptions and 144 real resumes, including Flutter, FastAPI, Golang, Redis, Docker, Kubernetes, and their relationships.

Figure \ref{fig:ontology} illustrates an example subgraph of the ontology, showing how skills are organized hierarchically and how partial-credit matching works across ontological distances.

\subsubsection{Traversal Algorithm}

For each required skill $s_j$ in the JD, the matcher finds the highest-scoring match among all resume skills $s_r \in S_R$ using the six-level weight scheme defined in Equation (3.1):

\begin{equation}
w(s_r, s_j) = \begin{cases}
1.0 & \text{if } s_r = s_j \text{ (exact match)} \\
0.9 & \text{if } s_r \text{ is an alias of } s_j \text{ (alias match)} \\
0.7 & \text{if } s_j \in \text{ancestors}(s_r, G) \text{ (child is more specific)} \\
0.5 & \text{if } s_r \in \text{ancestors}(s_j, G) \text{ (child is more general)} \\
0.4 & \text{if } \exists p \in V : p \in \text{ancestors}(s_r, G) \cap \text{ancestors}(s_j, G) \text{ (siblings)} \\
0.3 & \text{if shortest\_path}(s_r, s_j, G) = 2 \text{ (two-hop)} \\
0.0 & \text{otherwise (no connection)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The rationale for these weights reflects the semantic distance implied by each relationship. A candidate who lists ``pytorch'' is fully qualified in the sense of knowing a deep learning framework when the JD requires ``deep\_learning'' (0.7 --- child to parent), but only partially qualified when the JD specifically requires ``tensorflow'' (0.4 --- sibling, both are children of deep\_learning). This nuanced partial-credit system allows the pipeline to recognize competence where keyword matching would score zero.

The overall graph score is the mean of the best match weights across all JD skills:

\begin{equation}
\text{GraphScore}(R, J) = \frac{1}{|S_J|} \sum_{s_j \in S_J} \max_{s_r \in S_R} w(s_r, s_j)
\end{equation}

In addition to the scalar GraphScore, this computation yields four categorical counts: \texttt{exact\_matches}, \texttt{partial\_matches}, \texttt{no\_matches}, and \texttt{coverage} (the fraction of JD skills receiving any non-zero weight). These five values constitute the graph matcher's contribution to the feature vector.

\subsection{L4b — Semantic Matcher (Neural AI)}

\subsubsection{SBERT Architecture}

The semantic matcher employs Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019}, specifically the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} variant. SBERT is a Siamese BERT architecture that adds a pooling layer over BERT's output to produce a fixed-size sentence embedding. The \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} variant is a distilled model with 6 transformer layers and 384-dimensional embeddings, offering a favorable balance between semantic quality and inference speed.

The semantic similarity between resume $R$ and job description $J$ is computed as the cosine similarity between their SBERT embeddings:

\begin{equation}
\text{SBERT\_Score}(R, J) = \frac{\vec{v}_R \cdot \vec{v}_J}{\|\vec{v}_R\| \cdot \|\vec{v}_J\|}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{v}_R = \text{SBERT}(\text{text}(R))$ and $\vec{v}_J = \text{SBERT}(\text{text}(J))$.

Since \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} has a 256-token input limit (approximately 1,500 characters), long texts are truncated to the final 2,000 characters before encoding. The tail of the resume (which typically contains projects and technical skills) and the tail of the JD (which typically contains required qualifications) are thus prioritized over boilerplate header text.

\subsubsection{SBERT Fine-Tuning}

The base \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} was trained on general-purpose internet text corpora. While it provides reasonable semantic similarity for general English, it has not been exposed to the specific patterns of technical hiring language. Consider the following pair:

\begin{quote}
Resume: ``Implemented CI/CD pipelines using Jenkins and Docker for microservices deployment in a fintech startup.''\\
JD: ``Looking for a DevOps engineer with deployment automation and containerization experience.''
\end{quote}

A human hiring manager immediately recognizes this as a strong match. The base SBERT model, lacking domain context, may assign a lower-than-appropriate similarity because the surface vocabulary overlap is limited. Fine-tuning on domain-specific pairs teaches the model what ``similar'' means in a hiring context.

We fine-tune using \textbf{CosineSimilarityLoss}:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \cos(\vec{v}_{R_i}, \vec{v}_{J_i}) - y_i \right)^2
\end{equation}

where $y_i \in \{0.0, 1.0\}$ is the match label. For positive pairs ($y_i = 1.0$), the loss pushes the embeddings toward cosine similarity 1.0; for negative pairs ($y_i = 0.0$), it pushes them toward cosine similarity 0.0. After fine-tuning, the SBERT score becomes domain-specific: it measures not general English similarity but hiring-domain match.

\subsection{L4c — Structural Matcher}

The structural matcher captures candidate--job compatibility along dimensions that neither the ontology graph nor the semantic model can directly address: years of experience and educational qualifications.

\subsubsection{Years of Experience Score}

\begin{equation}
\text{YOE\_Score} = \max\left(0.0,\ 1.0 - \frac{\max(0,\ \text{YOE}_\text{req} - \text{YOE}_\text{resume})}{\text{YOE}_\text{req} + 1}\right)
\end{equation}

This formula produces 1.0 when the candidate meets or exceeds the YOE requirement, and decreases proportionally as the shortfall increases. The denominator ($\text{YOE}_\text{req} + 1$) prevents division by zero for roles with no stated YOE requirement.

\subsubsection{Education Score}

Education levels are mapped to ordinal ranks (High School=1, Diploma=2, B.Tech=3, M.Tech=4, PhD=5). The education score is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Edu\_Score} = \begin{cases}
1.0 & \text{if } \text{rank}_\text{resume} \geq \text{rank}_\text{req} \\
0.5 & \text{if } \text{rank}_\text{resume} = \text{rank}_\text{req} - 1 \\
0.0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Section Score}

\begin{equation}
\text{Section\_Score} = \frac{|\text{detected\_sections}|}{|\text{expected\_sections}|}
\end{equation}

where expected sections are Skills, Experience, Projects, and Education (4 sections total).

\subsubsection{Combined Structural Score}

\begin{equation}
\text{StructuralScore} = \frac{\text{YOE\_Score} + \text{Edu\_Score} + \text{Section\_Score}}{3}
\end{equation}

The structural matcher produces four features: \texttt{structural\_score}, \texttt{yoe\_score}, \texttt{edu\_score}, \texttt{section\_score}, plus two raw values: \texttt{resume\_yoe}, \texttt{required\_yoe}, \texttt{resume\_edu\_rank}, \texttt{required\_edu\_rank}.

\section{L5 — Classification and Explainability}

\subsection{Feature Vector}

The 14-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{x}$ passed to XGBoost is:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = [\underbrace{g_1, g_2, g_3, g_4, g_5}_{\text{graph}},\ \underbrace{s_1}_{\text{SBERT}},\ \underbrace{t_1, t_2, t_3, t_4, t_5, t_6, t_7, t_8}_{\text{structural + raw}}]
\end{equation}

where the graph features are \{graph\_score, coverage, exact\_matches, partial\_matches, no\_matches\}, the SBERT feature is \{sbert\_score\}, and the structural features are \{structural\_score, yoe\_score, edu\_score, section\_score, resume\_yoe, required\_yoe, resume\_edu\_rank, required\_edu\_rank\}.

\subsection{XGBoost Classifier}

XGBoost \cite{chen2016} (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an ensemble method that builds an additive model of decision trees, where each successive tree corrects the residual errors of its predecessors by following the gradient of the loss function:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}^{(t)} = \hat{y}^{(t-1)} + \eta \cdot f_t(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}

where $\hat{y}^{(t)}$ is the prediction after $t$ trees, $\eta$ is the learning rate, and $f_t$ is the $t$-th regression tree fitted to the negative gradient of the loss. The final prediction is the sigmoid of the sum over all trees, producing a probability in $[0, 1]$.

XGBoost was selected for three reasons: (1) it achieves state-of-the-art performance on tabular datasets with under 5,000 training samples; (2) it produces native SHAP-compatible tree structures; and (3) it trains in seconds on CPU, making it suitable for deployment without GPU infrastructure.

\subsection{SHAP Attribution}

After XGBoost produces its prediction, SHAP TreeExplainer computes exact Shapley values for each of the 14 features. Figure \ref{fig:shap} shows a representative SHAP attribution plot for a single prediction, illustrating which features pushed the classifier toward Match and which pushed it toward No Match.

\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf{x}) = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{14} \phi_i
\end{equation}

where $\phi_0$ is the base rate (mean training prediction) and $\phi_i$ is the Shapley value of feature $i$. Positive $\phi_i$ means feature $i$ pushed the prediction toward ``Match''; negative $\phi_i$ means it pushed toward ``No Match''. These values are displayed to the user as a horizontal bar chart.

\section{L6 — Explainer}

\subsection{Evidence Mapper}

For each skill $s_j \in S_J$ that was matched (i.e., received a non-zero graph weight), the evidence mapper finds the most relevant sentence in the resume. It computes TF-IDF weighted token overlap between the skill string and each sentence in the resume, returning the sentence with the highest score as the evidence sentence.

The user sees: ``Matched: \textit{machine\_learning} (via pytorch, child→parent) — Evidence: \textit{`Developed ML pipeline for credit risk scoring using PyTorch and scikit-learn.'}''

\subsection{Skill Gap Ranker}

For each unmatched skill $s_j \in S_J$ that received a graph weight of 0.0, the skill gap ranker computes a counterfactual impact score: how much would the overall match probability increase if this skill were added to the resume?

The impact is estimated by constructing a modified feature vector $\mathbf{x}'$ in which the missing skill is hypothetically added to the resume's skill set, recomputing the graph features, and computing the change in XGBoost predicted probability:

\begin{equation}
\Delta_j = P_\text{XGB}(\mathbf{x}'_j) - P_\text{XGB}(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}

Missing skills are ranked by $\Delta_j$ in descending order and presented to the user with links to learning resources from a curated JSON map (\texttt{learning\_resources.json}).

\section{L7 — Web Application}

The Flask web application provides the end-user interface. Key features include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Resume upload}: Drag-and-drop or click-to-upload interface accepting PDF and DOCX
  \item \textbf{JD input}: Free-text area for pasting job description text
  \item \textbf{Results dashboard}: Match verdict with confidence percentage, three score cards (graph / SBERT / structural), skill-by-skill breakdown with evidence sentences, ranked skill gap list with learning links, SHAP bar chart
  \item \textbf{Match history}: All previous analyses stored in SQLite via Flask-SQLAlchemy, accessible in a paginated history view
  \item \textbf{Authentication}: User registration and login via Flask-Login with bcrypt password hashing
  \item \textbf{Ontology visualizer}: Interactive skill graph explorer built with vis-network (JavaScript), allowing users to browse the 434-node ontology and understand skill relationships
  \item \textbf{Theme}: Dark/light mode toggle with CSS custom properties
\end{itemize}

\chapter{DATASET AND TRAINING}

\section{Data Collection Strategy}

The quality of a supervised machine learning system is fundamentally bounded by the quality of its training data. For resume--job matching, there are two central challenges: (1) labeled resume--JD pairs are not publicly available at scale, because most recruitment data is proprietary; and (2) generic public datasets (such as the Kaggle resume dataset) do not capture the specific distribution of Indian technical hiring contexts.

We addressed both challenges by assembling a multi-source dataset combining synthetic edge cases, a publicly available resume collection, and a proprietary dataset of real resumes from our own peer network matched against real industry job descriptions.

\section{Dataset Sources}

\subsection{Synthetic Pairs}

We manually constructed 167 resume--JD pairs to cover edge cases and boundary conditions that are unlikely to appear naturally in collected data:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Semantic equivalence pairs}: Resume and JD use entirely different terminology for the same concept (e.g., ``API development'' vs. ``REST endpoint engineering''), testing the SBERT component's ability to recognize equivalent meaning
  \item \textbf{Near-miss negative pairs}: Resume is superficially similar to JD (e.g., both mention Python) but is clearly not a match (e.g., data analyst resume for software engineering role), testing the system's ability to avoid false positives
  \item \textbf{Ontology boundary cases}: Resume has a skill that is a sibling of the required skill, testing the 0.4-weight sibling matching behavior
  \item \textbf{Experience gap pairs}: Candidate has all required skills but insufficient YOE, testing the structural component
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kaggle Resume Dataset}

The Kaggle Resume Dataset (Anbhawal, 2020) contains 800 real resumes across 24 job categories. We used the category labels to construct resume--JD pairs: resumes were matched against job descriptions from their own category (positive pairs) and from randomly sampled different categories (negative pairs). This dataset provides breadth across job categories but lacks the depth of real industry JD text.

\subsection{Friends Dataset}

This is the most valuable and novel component of our training data. We collected 144 real B.Tech student resumes from our peer network, spanning students from multiple institutions and covering a range of technical specializations. Against these 144 resumes, we matched 17 real job descriptions manually collected from Naukri and LinkedIn. The 17 JDs cover roles including:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Full Stack Developer (React + Node.js)
  \item Data Scientist (Python, ML, SQL)
  \item Backend Engineer (Java, Spring Boot)
  \item Flutter Developer (Dart, Firebase)
  \item Machine Learning Engineer (Python, TensorFlow, MLOps)
  \item Java Backend Developer (Java, REST APIs, MySQL)
  \item MERN Stack Developer (MongoDB, Express, React, Node.js)
  \item Golang Developer (Go, microservices, Docker)
  \item DevOps Engineer (Docker, Kubernetes, CI/CD)
  \item Android Developer (Java/Kotlin, Android SDK)
\end{itemize}

The full cross-product of 144 resumes $\times$ 17 JDs yields 2,448 pairs. Feature extraction was run on all pairs, and auto-labeling was applied based on the extracted signal thresholds (described in Section 4.3), yielding 887 usable labeled pairs.

\section{Labeling Methodology}

\subsection{Manual Labeling (Synthetic Pairs)}

All 167 synthetic pairs were manually labeled by the project team, with each pair reviewed independently by two team members. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and majority vote.

\subsection{Score-Based Auto-Labeling (Friends Dataset)}

For the 887 pairs from the friends dataset, manual labeling at scale was not feasible. We applied a threshold-based auto-labeling rule grounded in the intuition that two independent matching signals agreeing strongly is a reliable indicator of the true label:

\begin{equation}
\text{label}(R, J) = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \text{GraphScore}(R,J) > 0.4 \text{ AND } \text{SBERT\_Score}(R,J) > 0.4 \\
0 & \text{if } \text{GraphScore}(R,J) < 0.3 \text{ AND } \text{SBERT\_Score}(R,J) < 0.3 \\
-1 & \text{otherwise (excluded from training)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This conservative thresholding approach produced 636 positive pairs (label=1) and 251 negative pairs (label=0), for a total of 887 labeled pairs. The 1,561 ``mixed'' pairs (where the two signals disagreed) were excluded from training to avoid label noise. The resulting label distribution is imbalanced (71.7\% positive, 28.3\% negative), reflecting the fact that most B.Tech resumes have at least some overlap with the technical JDs they were matched against.

\section{SBERT Fine-Tuning}

\subsection{Training Setup}

Fine-tuning was conducted using the sentence-transformers library. The 887 labeled pairs were converted to \texttt{InputExample} objects with the resume text, JD text, and label (0.0 or 1.0 as float). A \texttt{DataLoader} with \texttt{batch\_size=16} was constructed and \texttt{CosineSimilarityLoss} was used as the training objective.

Training hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Base model: \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}
  \item Epochs: 4
  \item Batch size: 16
  \item Warmup steps: 88 (10\% of total training steps)
  \item Optimizer: AdamW (default sentence-transformers configuration)
  \item Total training steps: 887 / 16 $\times$ 4 = 222 steps
  \item Final training loss: 0.069
\end{itemize}

The fine-tuned model was saved to \texttt{models/sbert\_finetuned/}. The pipeline's semantic matcher automatically loads this model when it exists, falling back to the base model if it does not.

\section{XGBoost Training}

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

For all 1,854 labeled pairs, the complete 14-feature vector was extracted by running each pair through layers L1--L4 of the pipeline. This feature extraction was performed offline and cached in \texttt{data/annotation.csv} to decouple the classifier training from the full pipeline execution.

\subsection{Cross-Validation}

Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, which ensures that the positive/negative class ratio is preserved in each fold. \chapter{EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS}

\section{Experimental Setup}

All experiments were conducted on a consumer-grade laptop with an Intel Core i5 processor, 16 GB RAM, and no GPU. This was a deliberate design choice: we wanted to demonstrate that the full ExplainHire pipeline is deployable without specialized hardware. SBERT fine-tuning ran on CPU using the \texttt{accelerate} library and completed in approximately 15 minutes for 4 epochs over 887 pairs.

All reported F1 scores are macro-averaged F1 computed using scikit-learn's \texttt{f1\_score} with \texttt{average="macro"} and \texttt{zero\_division=0}. We report macro F1 rather than weighted F1 because the classes are imbalanced, and macro F1 penalizes poor performance on the minority class.

\textbf{Analysis of results:}

The TF-IDF baseline (macro F1 = 0.037) confirms that keyword co-occurrence is nearly useless for this task. The extreme low score reflects the many near-miss cases where resumes and JDs discuss the same domain using different vocabulary.

SBERT-only (F1 = 0.484) represents a substantial improvement over TF-IDF, demonstrating that semantic embedding captures meaningful signal. However, at roughly chance performance for a two-class problem, SBERT alone is insufficient. The likely reason is that global document similarity is an imprecise proxy for skill-by-skill match: a resume from a data scientist will be semantically similar to an ML engineer JD, but may lack specific required skills.

Graph-only (F1 = 0.747) is the strongest single-signal configuration and the most informative result of the ablation study. It demonstrates that structured skill matching over an ontology is substantially more informative than either keyword matching or global semantic similarity. This validates the core architectural decision to build a skill ontology.

Graph + SBERT (F1 = 0.832) improves further, confirming that the neural and symbolic signals are complementary rather than redundant. SBERT captures contextual fit that the graph cannot (e.g., overall career narrative alignment), while the graph provides structured skill-level precision that SBERT cannot (e.g., distinguishing partial from exact skill matches).

Full pipeline (F1 = 0.951) achieves the highest performance, with structural features contributing +11.9\% over the Graph + SBERT baseline. This is a large improvement attributable to structural features, indicating that years of experience and education level carry significant independent predictive power. A candidate can be a strong semantic match for a job but lack the required experience --- the structural matcher captures this directly.

\chapter{DISCUSSION}

\section{Why Each Component Matters}

The ablation results reveal a clear hierarchy of signal importance: Graph (0.747) > SBERT (0.484) > Structural (contributes +11.9\% at the margin). This ordering has practical implications.

The ontology graph is the most valuable single component because resume--job matching is fundamentally a structured problem: a job requires specific skills, a resume has specific skills, and the question is whether the candidate's skills cover the job's requirements. A structured knowledge graph is the natural representation for this kind of hierarchical coverage checking. The graph's 6-level traversal means it can credit a candidate who has a related but not identical skill, which is exactly the kind of judgment a human reviewer would make.

SBERT contributes substantially as the second signal, capturing contextual alignment that the skill-by-skill graph cannot. However, SBERT alone underperforms the graph because global document similarity is an imprecise proxy for structured skill coverage: two documents can be highly semantically similar without the resume containing the specific skills required by the JD. The graph catches this; SBERT does not.

Structural features add the most value at the margin, suggesting that years of experience and education level provide information orthogonal to both skill signals. This makes intuitive sense: it is entirely possible to have all the required skills but insufficient professional experience, or the right skills and experience but not the required degree level. The structural features capture these dimensions directly.

\section{Comparison with Large Language Models}

A natural question is whether ExplainHire's approach is competitive with modern large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Claude, or Gemini, which can be prompted to assess resume--JD fit.

\textbf{Where LLMs have advantages}: Large language models have broader world knowledge, can read career narratives and extract implicit signals (e.g., infer technical level from project descriptions), and can handle unusual or ambiguous cases through reasoning.

\textbf{Where ExplainHire has advantages}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: ExplainHire produces the same output for the same input every time. LLMs produce different outputs across runs, making them unsuitable for fair, auditable employment screening.
  \item \textbf{Cost and speed}: A single LLM inference for a long resume + JD pair costs money and takes seconds. ExplainHire runs in milliseconds at essentially zero marginal cost. At the scale of thousands of applications per role, this difference is decisive.
  \item \textbf{Structured explainability}: ExplainHire's explanations are structured data: specific matched skills, specific evidence sentences, specific SHAP values. LLM explanations are natural language text that may be fluent but internally inconsistent and non-reproducible.
  \item \textbf{Auditability}: For employment law compliance, decisions need to be auditable. ExplainHire's SHAP values and ontology traversal paths constitute an audit trail. An LLM's chain of thought does not.
\end{itemize}

ExplainHire is not competing with LLMs for the task of nuanced text understanding. It is competing with ATS systems for the task of structured, explainable, consistent, scalable employment screening. On that framing, it wins clearly.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Dataset scope}: Our training data is biased toward Indian B.Tech students in software engineering roles. Performance on resumes from other educational backgrounds, career stages (senior, executive), or non-technical domains has not been evaluated and may be lower.

\textbf{Auto-labeling noise}: The 887 friends-dataset pairs were auto-labeled by threshold rules. While conservative thresholds reduce noise, some labels may be incorrect, introducing training signal errors.

\textbf{Ontology coverage}: The 434-node ontology covers the roles in our 17 job descriptions well but will have gaps for other technical domains. Any JD skill not present in the ontology or alias table will receive a graph score of 0 regardless of whether a semantically equivalent skill is in the resume.

\textbf{YOE extraction reliability}: Year-of-experience extraction by regex is inherently imperfect. Resumes that state experience implicitly (``worked from 2021 to present'') rather than explicitly (``3 years of experience'') may have their YOE underestimated.

\section{Future Work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ontology expansion}: Extending the skill graph to cover non-technical domains (finance, law, healthcare) and more fine-grained technology skill hierarchies
  \item \textbf{Active learning}: Using the counterfactual skill gap ranker to identify which unlabeled pairs would be most informative to label manually, improving training data quality
  \item \textbf{Multi-lingual support}: Adding Hindi and regional Indian language support for resume parsing, reflecting the linguistic reality of many Indian job seekers
  \item \textbf{Candidate ranking}: Extending from binary classification to ranked candidate scoring across a full applicant pool
  \item \textbf{LLM integration}: Exploring whether LLM-based narrative understanding can be integrated as a fourth signal alongside graph, SBERT, and structural
\end{itemize}

\chapter{CONCLUSION}

This report has presented \textbf{ExplainHire}, a neurosymbolic artificial intelligence system for explainable resume--job description matching. The system was designed to address two fundamental failures of current ATS technology: semantic blindness (the inability to match semantically equivalent skill descriptions that use different surface forms) and decision opacity (the complete absence of structured explanation for screening outcomes).

ExplainHire's seven-layer pipeline combines a 434-node symbolic skill ontology graph derived from O*NET, a domain-adapted SBERT sentence embedding model fine-tuned on 887 real resume--JD pairs, and structural features capturing years of experience and education level. These three signals are fused by an XGBoost classifier trained on 1,854 labeled pairs, with SHAP TreeExplainer providing feature attribution and a sentence-level evidence mapper providing skill-by-skill justification.

The key findings of this work are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{All three signals are necessary}. The TF-IDF baseline achieves F1 = 0.037; the full pipeline achieves F1 = 0.951. No single signal approaches this level of performance alone.

  \item \textbf{The symbolic ontology graph is the most valuable single component} (F1 = 0.747 alone), outperforming the neural SBERT signal (F1 = 0.484), confirming that structured domain knowledge cannot be replaced by statistical language models for skill matching.

  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning SBERT on real recruitment data is important}. The domain-adapted SBERT model contributes more effectively than the base model and enables the system to capture semantic equivalences specific to technical hiring language.

  \item \textbf{Structural features add substantial value at the margin} (+11.9\% F1 over Graph + SBERT), demonstrating that non-skill signals carry significant independent predictive power.

  \item \textbf{ExplainHire generalizes to public benchmarks}. On ResuméAtlas (Heakl et al., 2024), ExplainHire achieves F1 = 0.720, outperforming the reported TF-IDF baseline by +11.06\%.

  \item \textbf{Explainability is achieved without sacrificing accuracy}. ExplainHire is simultaneously the most accurate and the only explainable system among all comparable published works. Explainability and accuracy are not in tension in this architecture --- they reinforce each other.
\end{enumerate}

ExplainHire demonstrates that the neurosymbolic paradigm --- combining structured symbolic knowledge with learned neural representations --- is a productive approach for high-stakes natural language understanding tasks where both accuracy and explainability are required. The recruitment domain is one of many such tasks; similar architectures could be applied to medical diagnosis, legal document review, and other domains where decisions must be both accurate and accountable.

The complete system, including the trained models, ontology, training data pipeline, evaluation scripts, and Flask web application, is available in the project repository.
